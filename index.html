Part I · Foundations

What the midterm actually covers

Read this once. It tells you what to optimise for, and what not to.

Midterm scope

Everything in Chapter 1. On slide 44, the twelve common operations, your professor will examine 10 of the 12, without saying which. So all twelve have to be learned; none can be dropped.

That instruction is more useful than it first looks. It means no single mechanism is worth more than any other, and it means the two you skip are the two that appear. The efficient response is not to guess. It is to get every mechanism to the point where it costs you no thinking time, which is what Part IV is built for.

Beyond the midterm, your grade is one 120-minute written exam in January worth 100%, with nothing on the desk but a calculator. The whole course in one line

A business event becomes a journal entry, which lands in T-accounts, whose balances are copied into the balance sheet and the income statement.

Every exercise you will be given is one of those four steps, or the chain end to end. "Do I book this?" is step one. "Which side?" is step two. "What is the closing value of the account?" is step three. "Rebuild the balance sheet" is step four. When a question confuses you, first identify which step it is asking about.

Company forms and who answers for what

This decides which rules apply for the rest of the course.

Switzerland recognises several legal forms; this course cares about profit-oriented ones, and really about three. More than half of all Swiss companies are sole proprietorships (53%), with Sàrl at 22% and SA at 20%.

The form matters because it sets what the capital is called, whether personal wealth is at risk, and whether the owner's account is equity or a liability, which is the one you will be tested on.

Who reports to whom

Sàrl and SA are organised identically (art. 620ss and 772ss CO). Three organs, and the direction of the arrows is the examinable part.

1 · Shareholders' General Assembly

Owns the company, holds supreme power. Adopts the articles of association, appoints and dismisses the board, CEO and auditor, approves or rejects the annual report, the financial statements and the allocation of earnings, increases or decreases the share capital.

Nominates · must pay subscribed shares
2 · Board of Directors

Elected by the assembly, manages the company. Defines strategy, defines accounting practices and internal control, appoints and supervises the CEO, prepares the annual report, executes the assembly's decisions.

Notifies the court if over-indebted
3 · External auditor

Elected by the assembly, must stay independent. Checks that the statements and the allocation of earnings comply with the articles and the law, checks an internal control system exists, reports infringements.

Backstop if the board fails to notify
Examinable detail

The board prepares the annual report; the assembly approves it. The auditor never approves: it certifies or reports. And both the board and the auditor carry the duty to notify the court in a situation of over-indebtedness: the auditor's duty is the backstop if the board omits it.

Swiss accounting law, as three decisions

art. 957ss CO · the part of the deck that is really a flowchart

The Code of Obligations sets out accounting obligations, the definitions of assets, liabilities, expenses and revenue, the composition of the statements, valuation methods, reporting and audit duties, and the consequences of breaking the rules. Other laws fill in specifics: audit activity is governed by the LSR.

The deck spends eight slides on this. It is three questions.

Decision 1: must I keep full accounts?

Am I obliged to prepare legal annual accounts? (art. 957 CO)

Decision 2: which audit regime applies?

Which audit regime applies? (art. 727ss CO)
Opting-out

No audit at all. Accounts are never checked externally.

Average full-time employees < 10 AND all shareholders agree
Limited audit

Soft audit certifying no irregularities were found. The default.

Everything that is neither opting-out nor ordinary
Ordinary audit

Detailed audit certifying the full authenticity of the statements.

Listed · public bonds · consolidated · OR 2 of 3 for 2 consecutive years: BS > 20m · sales > 40m · > 250 FTE
Trap

Opting-out needs both conditions: under 10 FTE and unanimous agreement. A question giving only the headcount is incomplete on purpose. For the ordinary audit it is 2 of 3 thresholds in 2 consecutive years; one big year does not trigger it.

Decision 3: consolidate, and under which standard?

Consolidation (art. 963 CO): if company A controls company B, in practice more than 50% of the shares, A must merge both sets of statements and present them as one entity, the group "A+B". Small groups are exempt.

Standards (art. 963b CO): Swiss GAAP, IFRS and US GAAP. Compulsory for listed companies (Nestlé, Swatch, Roche), cooperatives with 2’000+ members (Migros, Coop, Mobility) and foundations required by law. They can also be demanded by 20% of shareholders, 10% of cooperative members, or 20% of association members.

The five principles you must be able to name

Clarity

The statements must be understandable.

Completeness

All transactions must be recorded.

Reliability

Figures and explanations accurate and truthful.

Carefulness

In doubt, take the most conservative option. This is the principle behind provisions and lower-of-cost-or-market.

Non-compensation

You may not offset assets against liabilities, nor revenue against expenses.

Preservation

art. 958f: statements, appendices and supporting documents kept 10 years.

Where non-compensation bites. It is why VAT due and VAT recoverable are two separate accounts during the period rather than one net figure, and why a provision on receivables is a liability account shown in deduction of the asset, rather than simply a smaller receivable.

Part II · The two statements

The balance sheet

art. 959-959a CO · a photograph at a single instant, e.g. 31.12.20XX

The balance sheet answers one question: what does the company have, and who has a claim on it? Three things: assets (resources controlled by the company that will generate future benefits), liabilities (debt obligations owed to third parties) and equity (wealth at the company's disposal for distribution and investment).

The two sides are equal by construction, not by coincidence. Everything owned was financed either by someone the company owes, or by its owners.

The two ordering rules

Assets in order of liquidity is how fast they turn into cash. Liabilities in order of exigibility is how fast they turn into a cash outflow. The 12-month line separates current from non-current on both sides.

Assets at acquisition cost

Not at what they are worth today. This is the source of hidden reserves, covered in Part V.

Liabilities at nominal value

The face amount owed, not a discounted value.

Classification traps that cost marks
  • Installments to suppliers = paid in advance = current asset. Installments from clients = received in advance = current liability. The preposition is the whole question.
  • Unpaid share capital is an asset, because shareholders owe you, even though capital is equity.
  • Accruals appear on both sides; the direction of the adjustment decides. See §9.
  • A provision on AR is technically a liability but is always presented in deduction of the receivable.

The link between the two statements

The net result computed by the P&L must be identical to the net result shown in the balance sheet. If they differ, something is unrecorded or misclassified. Exercise 4 is built on exactly this check.

The appendix (art. 959c CO)

Third mandatory component. It discloses the accounting principles used, details and comments on specific accounts, decreases of hidden reserves that improve the net result, and other legally required information. The cash flow statement and the statement of changes in equity are optional, mostly produced by large or listed companies.

Part III · The engine

Debit and credit, settled once

Get this right and roughly 80% of the exercises become arithmetic.

Double-entry means every operation touches at least two accounts, one on the debit (left) side and one on the credit (right), with equal totals. Debit and credit do not mean plus and minus. They mean left and right. Whether left is an increase depends on the type of account, and there are only four types, splitting two against two.

The memory hook

Expenses behave like assets. Revenue behaves like liabilities. Spending money and buying a machine both start on the left; earning revenue and taking a loan both start on the right.

That works because an expense is a resource you consumed instead of keeping, so it enters where an asset would. Revenue increases what the owners are owed, which is equity, so it enters where equity does. The four types are really two, mirrored.

1 · Value Added Tax

LTVA / OTVA · the single most common source of arithmetic errors in this course

VAT is a tax on private consumption. A company is only a collector: it charges VAT to customers, pays VAT to suppliers, and settles the difference with the Confederation. After federal income tax (35%), VAT is the Confederation's second largest income source at 33%.

The rates, and what triggers each
RateNameApplies to
8.1%NormalThe default: most goods and services, cars, restaurants, clothes
2.6%ReducedBasic goods: medicine, food (take-away), newspapers, books
3.8%SpecialThe housing industry: hotels, inns

Exonerated is not the same as excluded. Exonerated (0%): no VAT charged, but the company can still recover its input VAT. Excluded: outside the scope entirely, so no VAT charged and no input VAT recovery. On an exam, export sales are exonerated; a rental invoice is excluded.

Collecting VAT becomes compulsory at 100’000 CHF of sales over 12 months, applied proportionally, so 50’000 CHF in 6 months also triggers it. Below that, registration is voluntary.

Income, and the trap inside it

Dividends = rate % × nominal value  (share capital, not market price)
Coupons / interest = rate % × nominal value  (of the bonds)
Nominal, not market

A question giving a nominal value of 10 CHF, a market price of 60 CHF and a dividend rate of 4% is testing exactly one thing. The dividend is 4% × 10 per share. The market price of 60 is there to see whether you reach for it.

Accrued interest when bonds change hands mid-year

A bond pays its coupon once a year, to whoever holds it on the payment date. If you buy in between, you compensate the seller for the interest they earned but will not receive, then recover it when the full coupon arrives.

That compensation is booked to interest charges on purchase, so that when the full coupon is credited as interest revenue, only the portion you genuinely earned remains in the P&L.

Accrued interest = nominal × rate % × months ÷ 12
Two prices in one bond transaction

The quoted price, a percentage of nominal, buys the bond and goes to the asset account. The accrued interest is separate, and goes to interest charges on a purchase or interest revenue on a sale. Never merge them.

3 · Withholding tax

Loi sur l'Impôt Anticipé (LIA) · 35% skimmed at source, and recoverable

The Confederation levies withholding tax on specific financial income, at source, meaning the payer deducts it and you receive only the remainder. It applies to interest and coupons on bonds from Swiss entities, dividends on Swiss shares, interest on bank accounts above 200 CHF per year, money games and lotteries, and some insurance income.

4 · Provisions

art. 960e CO · a liability for a risk that has not yet materialised

The three-part test, all three must hold

Liability: a debt, a loss of value or a cash outflow is coming. Past event: the origin of the risk is already behind you. Probability: it is likely enough to occur, around 70% counting as probable.

The decisive one is almost always the middle test. No sign of risk in the past means no provision and no entry. A recession that might hurt next year's sales is a forecast, not a past event. A lawsuit already filed is a past event.

Note the asymmetry Swiss law allows: an unjustified provision may be maintained in the balance sheet. That is deliberate, and it is the legal doorway to hidden reserves in Part V.

5 · Inventory and cost of goods sold

art. 960c CO · two independent policy choices, often confused with each other

Inventory is a short-term asset holding tangible goods destined for customers: raw materials, semi-finished, finished, or merchandise bought to be resold. It is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value.

Cost includes
  • Materials
  • Transport: delivery, import taxes, tariffs
  • Production: salaries, depreciation, maintenance
  • Storage only between two stages of production, never passive storage
Net realisable value

The net expected sale price.

NRV = sale price − selling costs

Two separate decisions

A booking policy answers when the inventory account is touched. A valuation policy answers at what unit price. They are independent: any booking policy can pair with any valuation policy.

6 · The owner's account

Where business-unrelated transactions with owners go

Owners have basic rights (to vote, to earnings, to subscribe new shares in priority) and in practice specific privileges: free or discounted products, private expenses paid by the company such as rent, car or travel, private loans to or from the company, and interest on those loans.

Anything the owner takes or receives that is not business-related is booked to a dedicated owner's account, one per owner. Its balance says whether the owner owes the company money or the reverse.

The account in the official chart. In a raison individuelle it is 2850, prélèvements en espèces ou en nature (Privé). In a partnership each associate has a compte privé at 2820. The word "Privé" is the giveaway: this account exists precisely to separate private consumption from business activity.

Which side of the balance sheet

In a sole proprietorship, the owner's account sits inside equity, because the owner and the business are legally the same person. In a Sàrl or SA, it sits in liabilities, because the company is a separate legal person and genuinely owes or is owed.

8 · Financial debts

The amortization table, and why an annuity is not an expense

Financial instruments split three ways: equity instruments (shares, warrants, carrying ownership and earnings rights), debt instruments (bonds, loans, mortgages, carrying rights to contractual cash flows), and derivatives (forwards, futures, swaps, options, whose value depends on an underlying asset and which are outside this course).

A financial debt is a contractual obligation to repay a principal with interest over a period.

Secured debt

Granted against a collateral.

Unsecured debt

Granted on creditworthiness alone.

Revolving debt

A continuous line negotiated up to a limit.

Mortgage

For acquiring a property, which is itself the collateral.

Four terms define any debt: the principal owed, the interest rate compensating credit risk, the maturity (frequency and dates of payment), and the payment itself, usually an annuity.

The single most important idea here

An annuity is not an expense. It splits into interest, a P&L expense computed on the current remaining debt, and amortization, a balance sheet repayment. Only the amortization reduces the principal.

Interest

Goes to the P&L as a financial expense. Computed on the debt still outstanding at the start of the period, so it shrinks every year.

Amortization

Goes to the balance sheet, reducing the debt. Grows every year as the interest portion shrinks.

= Annuity

The constant instalment actually paid. Same total each year, different split each year.

Physical bills and coins, changed at the desk.

Currency

A second P&L, built for decisions rather than for the law

The statutory P&L tells you what happened. Break-even analysis asks a different question, how much must we sell before we stop losing money?, and needs costs split not by nature or function but by behaviour: variable costs move with volume, fixed costs do not.

Hidden reserves

Absent from your Chapter 1 slides, yet Exercise 9 is entirely about it, and the course overview lists it under "accounting fundamentals"

Why this section exists. The Chapter 1 deck never mentions hidden reserves, yet Exercise 9 asks you to compute them, restate a balance sheet at fair value and journalise the transition. The concept follows directly from two rules you have already met, assets are recorded at acquisition cost and in doubt, be conservative, so it is written up here from first principles. Check the detail against your professor's own treatment when they cover it.

A hidden reserve is equity that exists economically but does not appear in the balance sheet. It arises whenever the books are deliberately more pessimistic than reality.

The definition, and the only formula you need

Hidden reserve = fair value − book value for an asset, and book value − fair value for a liability. In both cases: the amount by which the balance sheet understates the company's true wealth.

The four ways they are created

Undervaluing an asset

Inventory carried at 2/3 of what it would fetch. A building depreciated faster than it actually wears out.

Over-depreciating

Writing a machine down to 180’000 when it is genuinely worth 250’000.

Over-providing

A provision on AR larger than the real risk, or a provision for a liability that has shrunk but was never reduced.

Overvaluing a liability

Keeping a 300’000 provision on the books when the obligation is now 200’000.

Swiss law permits all of this. Recall art. 960e: unjustified provisions can be maintained in the balance sheet. The prudence principle deliberately allows management to understate. What the law demands in exchange is disclosure in the appendix (art. 959c) when a net dissolution of hidden reserves improves the reported result, because that would otherwise let a company invent a good year out of nothing.

The part that decides every exam answer

Hidden reserves increase

Creation

The books got more pessimistic this year

Book profit understated → ADD the increase
Hidden reserves decrease

Dissolution

The books got closer to reality this year

Book profit overstated → SUBTRACT the decrease
Result at fair value = result at book value + net increase in hidden reserves
Equity at fair value = equity at book value + total hidden reserves at the closing date
Two different quantities, do not mix them

The result is corrected by the variation over the year, a flow. Equity is corrected by the total stock of hidden reserves at the closing date. Using the total on the result, or the variation on equity, is the classic mistake, and both numbers are always given in the same table so the temptation is real.

A worked micro-example

Inventory is carried at 500’000 but is genuinely worth 750’000 at 01.01. A year later it is carried at 600’000 and worth 900’000.

The company created 50’000 of additional hidden reserve on inventory this year. Its reported profit is therefore 50’000 lower than the economic truth, and to restate you add 50’000 back.

Restating a whole balance sheet to fair value

The mechanical procedure, which is exactly what Exercise 9 part III asks for:

  1. For each position, compute the hidden reserve at both dates: FV − BV for assets, BV − FV for liabilities.
  2. Total them at each date. The difference between the totals is the year's variation.
  3. Journalise the revaluation position by position: debit each undervalued asset, debit each overvalued liability, crediting a "Dissolution of hidden reserves" account.
  4. Split that credit in two. The portion equal to the hidden reserves already existing at 01.01 belongs to prior years, so it goes to Reserves. The portion equal to the variation belongs to this year, so it goes to the Result of the period.

Your arithmetic check

Equity at fair value must equal fair-value assets minus fair-value liabilities. If that does not match book equity + total hidden reserves, one of your position-level reserves has the wrong sign. Exercise 9 lets you run this check, and you should.

Commercially, hidden reserves are a smoothing device. In a good year management over-depreciates and over-provides, hiding profit and reducing the tax bill. In a bad year they quietly dissolve those reserves and the reported result looks stable. Swiss law tolerates this, which is precisely why the appendix disclosure exists, and why an auditor's report and a stricter standard like Swiss GAAP or IFRS matter to an outside investor.

The traps

Ranked by how often they appear in the material you have been given.

1 · Dividing by the rate instead of 1 + rate

A price including 8.1% VAT is divided by 1.081. This appears in almost every exercise, so a habit here is worth more than any other single fix.

2 · Forgetting the reversal on a disposal

Indirect depreciation means two entries: the sale, then accumulated depreciation against the asset. Exercises 7 and 8 both test it.

3 · Not reversing accruals on 1 January

An accrual is temporary. If an exercise opens with an accrual on the balance sheet, your second entry is its cancellation.

4 · Dividends on market price

Always the nominal value. The market price in the question is bait.

5 · Booking the target, not the movement

"Increase the provision to 10’000" from 8’000 means an entry of 2’000.

6 · Reading a rate from your own side

Rates are the bank's. You buying foreign currency means the bank sells, so take the ask. If the rate you chose favours the company, it is the wrong one.

7 · "Margin included" as a mark-up

Margin of 1/3 included means cost is 2/3 of the selling price, not 3/4.

8 · Variation versus stock

On hidden reserves, the result takes the variation and equity takes the total.

9 · Booking an order or a facility

An order, a signed lease and an unused overdraft limit are all nothing. But a loan actually granted is booked, because the funds arrive.

10 · Interest on the original principal

In an amortization table, interest is always on the remaining debt. Recompute every row.

11 · Recording an expense twice

Paying an invoice that was already booked moves AP and bank only. No new expense.

12 · Removing a sold asset at sale price

An asset always leaves at its book value. The difference against the price is the gain or loss.

In the exam, when you are stuck

Write the entry you are sure of first, usually the cash or bank line, because you know exactly how much money moved. Then ask what the money was for. The gain, loss or unknown account is almost always the balancing figure.

Two things are worth noticing. First, almost every later chapter reuses a mechanism from Part IV: restructuring is hidden reserves plus provisions, earnings allocation is withholding tax plus reserves, M&A is participations plus consolidation. The foundation you build now is not thrown away. Second, the course overview names interpreting cash flow statements as an explicit learning objective, so it will be examined even though Swiss law lists it as optional.

A study plan that fits the calendar

Before the midterm, work Parts III and IV until the entries are automatic, because that is where the marks are, and remember that 10 of the 12 operations will appear. Redo Exercises 5, 6 and 8 without looking. After the midterm the new material arrives fast and assumes all of this, so the goal now is to make Chapter 1 cost you no thinking time later.
